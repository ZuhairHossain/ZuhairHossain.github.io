Being selected as a reviewer for **ACM CHI 2024** - the premier conference in Human-Computer Interaction - was both humbling and eye-opening. As an early-career researcher without a PhD, I wasn't sure what to expect. Here's what I learned.

## The Review Process

CHI uses a rigorous multi-stage review process. For each paper, I had to:

1. **Read deeply** - not just skim, but engage with every claim, method, and finding
2. **Assess contribution** - Does this advance our understanding? Is it novel?
3. **Evaluate methodology** - Are the user studies well-designed? Is the analysis sound?
4. **Provide constructive feedback** - Even for rejected papers, the goal is to help authors improve

I reviewed papers, case studies, and alt.chi submissions - each with different expectations and evaluation criteria.

## What Surprised Me

**The diversity of methods.** HCI papers use everything from controlled lab experiments to ethnographic fieldwork, from machine learning to speculative design. No single methodology dominates, and the best papers often combine approaches creatively.

**How much I learned.** Reviewing forced me to read work outside my comfort zone. I encountered research on accessible gaming, algorithmic auditing, and VR therapy - topics I wouldn't have sought out on my own.

**The responsibility.** Behind every paper is months (or years) of someone's work. Writing a thoughtful review is a responsibility I took seriously.

## Advice for Early-Career Researchers

If you get the opportunity to review:

- **Say yes.** It's the best way to understand what makes a strong paper
- **Be specific.** "This is weak" helps no one. Explain *why* and suggest alternatives
- **Be kind.** You can be rigorous without being harsh
- **Read the review guidelines carefully.** Each venue has its own expectations

## Beyond CHI

After CHI, I also reviewed for **ACM CUI '24**, **ACM DIS '24**, and **icSoftComp** (2022–2024). Each conference taught me something different about the standards and expectations of different research communities.

Reviewing hasn't just made me a better evaluator - it's made me a better researcher. I now approach my own work with the same critical eye I apply to others'.
