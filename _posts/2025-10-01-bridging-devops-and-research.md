People often ask me: *"You're a DevOps engineer AND a researcher? How do those connect?"* It's a fair question. On the surface, managing Kubernetes clusters and studying depression screening seem worlds apart. But the overlap runs deeper than you'd think.

## Systems Thinking

DevOps taught me to think in **systems**. When I design a CI/CD pipeline, I consider: How do components interact? Where are the failure points? How does a change in one part cascade through the whole?

HCI research requires the same thinking. When I study how a USSD-based service (**NavConnect**) impacts boatmen's livelihoods, I'm mapping a system: technology, users, socioeconomic context, cultural norms, infrastructure constraints. Change one variable, and everything shifts.

## Automation Mindset → Reproducible Research

In DevOps, we automate everything. Infrastructure as code. Automated testing. Reproducible deployments. This mindset directly translates to research:

- **Reproducible data analysis** - scripted pipelines, not manual Excel work
- **Version-controlled experiments** - tracking every change to your study design
- **Documentation** - if it's not documented, it doesn't exist (true for both servers and research)

## Working Under Pressure

Production outages at 2 AM taught me to stay calm, diagnose systematically, and communicate clearly under pressure. These same skills are invaluable during:

- Thesis defense presentations
- Conference Q&A sessions
- Tight paper submission deadlines

## Infrastructure for Vulnerable Populations

My DevOps work at RedDot Digital (a subsidiary of Robi Axiata) gave me firsthand experience with the **digital infrastructure** that serves millions. I saw how platform decisions - uptime, accessibility, latency - directly impact users, especially those in rural or underserved areas.

This experience informs my HCI research: I don't just study user interfaces in isolation. I think about the **infrastructure** those interfaces run on, and whether that infrastructure is equitable.

## The Unique Perspective

When I apply for PhD positions, I don't hide my industry experience - I lean into it. I bring:

- **Technical depth** (Docker, Kubernetes, CI/CD, cloud infrastructure)
- **Production-scale thinking** (real systems, real users, real constraints)
- **Research rigor** (published papers, thesis work, grant-funded projects)

This combination is rare, and I believe it positions me to tackle research problems that require both **technical skill and human understanding**.

For anyone navigating a similar path between industry and academia - embrace the duality. It's a strength, not a contradiction.
